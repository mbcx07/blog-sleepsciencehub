The Overlooked Sleep Disorder: Upper Airway Resistance Syndrome (UARS) and Its Impact on Sleep Quality

2026-07-13 · Sleep Disorders · Tags: sleep disorders, The, sleepsciencehub

For decades, the medical community has focused on obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) as the primary culprit behind fragmented sleep and daytime fatigue. But a quieter, more insidious condition lurks in the shadows of sleep medicine—one that often goes undiagnosed for years, leaving millions of people feeling perpetually exhausted without a clear explanation. This condition is Upper Airway Resistance Syndrome, or UARS. Unlike classic sleep apnea, UARS doesn't always present with dramatic pauses in breathing or loud gasping. Instead, it manifests as subtle, repeated arousals from sleep that fragment your rest and wreak havoc on your daytime energy, mood, and cognitive function. In this article, we'll explore the science behind UARS, why it's so frequently missed, and—most importantly—how you can recognize it and take actionable steps toward better sleep.

What Is Upper Airway Resistance Syndrome?

Upper Airway Resistance Syndrome is a sleep-related breathing disorder characterized by increased resistance to airflow in the upper airway during sleep. This resistance leads to partial airway obstruction, which triggers a cascade of physiological responses: your brain senses the increased effort required to breathe, and it briefly awakens you—often for just a few seconds—to restore normal airflow. These micro-arousals can occur dozens or even hundreds of times per night, but they are so brief that you may not remember them. The result is a night of sleep that feels unrefreshing, even if you spend eight hours in bed.

The term UARS was first coined in the early 1990s by Dr. Christian Guilleminault, a pioneer in sleep medicine, who noticed that some patients had symptoms similar to sleep apnea but did not meet the criteria for apnea or hypopnea. Instead, they exhibited increased respiratory effort and frequent arousals, yet their oxygen levels remained normal. This distinction is crucial: while sleep apnea involves actual pauses in breathing (apneas) or significant reductions in airflow (hypopneas), UARS is defined by a pattern of increasing respiratory effort that leads to arousals without meeting the threshold for apnea or hypopnea.

From a physiological standpoint, UARS is often linked to anatomical factors such as a narrow airway, enlarged tonsils, or a recessed jaw. But it can also be influenced by nasal congestion, obesity, or even the position you sleep in. The key is that the airway becomes partially obstructed, and your body has to work harder to breathe. This increased work triggers the brain's arousal system, which disrupts the natural progression of sleep stages, particularly deep sleep and REM sleep.

How UARS Differs from Obstructive Sleep Apnea

Many people assume that if they don't have classic sleep apnea, they don't have a sleep-related breathing disorder. But UARS occupies a distinct niche on the spectrum of sleep-disordered breathing. The primary difference lies in the severity of airflow reduction and the presence of oxygen desaturation. In OSA, you experience complete or near-complete airway collapse, leading to a cessation of airflow for at least 10 seconds, often accompanied by a drop in blood oxygen levels. In UARS, the airway narrows but doesn't fully collapse, so airflow is reduced but not completely blocked. Oxygen levels typically remain above 90%, and the events are shorter and more frequent.

This distinction has significant clinical implications. Because UARS doesn't show up on standard sleep studies as clearly as OSA, it's often missed. A typical polysomnogram (sleep study) measures the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI), which counts the number of apneas and hypopneas per hour. If your AHI is below 5, you're considered normal. But in UARS, your AHI might be low, yet you still experience frequent arousals due to increased respiratory effort. This is why many UARS patients are told they "don't have sleep apnea" and are sent home with no answers.

Another key difference is the demographic profile. OSA is more common in men and in people who are overweight or obese. UARS, on the other hand, tends to affect younger, thinner individuals—often women—who may not fit the typical sleep apnea stereotype. This further contributes to underdiagnosis, as clinicians may not even consider a breathing disorder in a healthy-looking patient.

Recognizing the Subtle Symptoms of UARS

The symptoms of UARS are often vague and overlap with other conditions like insomnia, anxiety, or chronic fatigue syndrome. This makes it particularly challenging to identify. If you're experiencing any of the following, it's worth considering UARS as a potential underlying cause:

  • Chronic fatigue and daytime sleepiness despite getting adequate hours of sleep
  • Frequent nighttime awakenings, often with a feeling of gasping or choking
  • Restless sleep, tossing and turning, or waking up with a dry mouth or sore throat
  • Morning headaches or a feeling of unrefreshing sleep
  • Difficulty concentrating, memory lapses, or brain fog during the day
  • Mood disturbances, including irritability, anxiety, or depression
  • Teeth grinding (bruxism) or jaw clenching, which can be a response to airway resistance
  • Insomnia-like symptoms, such as difficulty falling back asleep after waking

What makes UARS particularly insidious is that the arousals are so brief that you may not consciously register them. You might think you slept through the night, but your brain was actually cycling through micro-wakefulness events. Over time, this fragmented sleep leads to a chronic state of sympathetic nervous system activation—your body remains in a low-grade "fight or flight" mode, which can elevate blood pressure, increase heart rate, and contribute to systemic inflammation.

If you've tried conventional insomnia treatments, such as sleep hygiene improvements or cognitive behavioral therapy, and still feel exhausted, UARS might be the missing piece. It's also worth noting that UARS can coexist with other sleep disorders, such as insomnia or restless legs syndrome, further complicating the clinical picture.

The Diagnostic Challenge: Why UARS Is Often Missed

One of the biggest hurdles in diagnosing UARS is that standard sleep studies are not designed to detect it. The traditional polysomnogram focuses on measuring apneas, hypopneas, and oxygen desaturations. To identify UARS, you need a more sensitive measurement: the respiratory effort-related arousal (RERA). A RERA is defined as a sequence of breaths lasting at least 10 seconds, characterized by increasing respiratory effort, leading to an arousal from sleep, but not meeting the criteria for an apnea or hypopnea. Unfortunately, not all sleep labs score RERAs, and many still rely solely on the AHI.

If you suspect UARS, it's essential to seek out a sleep specialist who is familiar with this condition. When you undergo a sleep study, ask specifically whether RERAs will be scored. Some labs use esophageal manometry to measure the pressure changes in your chest, which is the gold standard for detecting increased respiratory effort. However, this is invasive and not routinely performed. A more common approach is to use a nasal pressure transducer and an effort belt, which can detect subtle changes in airflow and respiratory effort.

Another diagnostic tool is the home sleep apnea test (HSAT), but these are even less sensitive for UARS. They typically only measure airflow, oxygen levels, and heart rate, and they don't capture brain waves or arousals. Therefore, an in-lab polysomnogram with RERA scoring is the most reliable way to confirm UARS. If you've had a sleep study that came back "normal" but you still feel terrible, don't hesitate to ask for a re-evaluation or a second opinion.

Treatment Strategies: From Positional Therapy to CPAP and Beyond

Once UARS is diagnosed, the treatment approach is similar to that of sleep apnea, but with some nuances. The goal is to keep your airway open throughout the night, reducing the effort required to breathe and preventing those micro-arousals. Here are the most evidence-based strategies:

Positional Therapy: For many people, UARS is worse when sleeping on the back, as gravity can cause the tongue and soft palate to collapse backward. Sleeping on your side can significantly reduce airway resistance. You can use a body pillow or a specialized positional device to encourage side-sleeping. Some people find that elevating the head of the bed by a few inches also helps.

Oral Appliances: A mandibular advancement device (MAD) is a custom-fitted dental appliance that holds your lower jaw forward, which helps keep the airway open. This is often an effective treatment for mild to moderate UARS, especially if you have a receding jaw or dental crowding. A sleep dentist can fit you for one, and it's a non-invasive option that many patients tolerate well.

Continuous Positive Airway Pressure (CPAP): While CPAP is the gold standard for OSA, it can also be used for UARS. The key is to use a lower pressure setting, as the goal is not to splint the airway open against a complete collapse, but to reduce the resistance. Some patients find that CPAP is too uncomfortable, but newer machines with auto-adjusting pressure and heated humidification can improve comfort. If you're prescribed CPAP for UARS, work closely with your sleep specialist to fine-tune the settings.

Nasal Breathing and Allergy Management: Nasal congestion can exacerbate airway resistance. Using saline rinses, nasal steroid sprays, or even nasal dilator strips can improve airflow through the nose, which reduces the effort needed to breathe. If you have allergies, addressing them with antihistamines or immunotherapy can make a significant difference.

Lifestyle Modifications: Weight loss, if you're overweight, can reduce the amount of soft tissue in the airway. Avoiding alcohol and sedatives before bed is crucial, as these relax the muscles of the throat and increase resistance. Smoking cessation is also important, as smoking causes inflammation and fluid retention in the upper airway.

Myofunctional Therapy: This is a series of exercises that strengthen the muscles of the tongue, soft palate, and upper airway. Some studies have shown that myofunctional therapy can reduce the severity of sleep-disordered breathing by improving muscle tone. It's not a standalone cure, but it can be a valuable adjunct to other treatments.

It's important to note that treatment for UARS is not one-size-fits-all. You may need to try a combination of approaches to find what works best for you. Regular follow-up with your sleep specialist is essential to monitor your progress and adjust your treatment plan.

Conclusion

Upper Airway Resistance Syndrome is a hidden thief of sleep, silently robbing you of restorative rest and leaving you to grapple with unexplained fatigue, brain fog, and mood disturbances. Because it doesn't fit neatly into the classic sleep apnea paradigm, it's often overlooked, but that doesn't mean it's untreatable. By understanding the subtle signs, advocating for a comprehensive sleep study that includes RERA scoring, and exploring the range of treatment options available, you can reclaim the deep, uninterrupted sleep your body craves. If you've been struggling with chronic exhaustion and have hit a wall with conventional insomnia treatments, consider asking your doctor about UARS. Your journey to better sleep might just begin with a closer look at the resistance in your airway.
