When Insomnia and Sleep Apnea Collide: The Science of COMISA and How to Break the Cycle

2026-08-09 · Sleep Disorders · Tags: sleepsciencehub, sleep disorders

For decades, insomnia and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) have been treated as separate conditions, each with its own diagnostic criteria and treatment protocols. But the reality is far more complex. A growing body of research reveals that these two disorders frequently coexist, creating a condition known as COMISA (Comorbid Insomnia and Sleep Apnea). Far from being a rare overlap, COMISA affects a significant portion of sleep disorder patients, yet it often goes unrecognized and undertreated. When insomnia and sleep apnea collide, they don't just add their symptoms together—they amplify each other, creating a vicious cycle that can devastate sleep quality, daytime functioning, and long-term health. This article explores the science behind COMISA, why it's so often missed, and—most importantly—how you can break the cycle with evidence-based strategies.

What Is COMISA? More Than Just Two Diagnoses

COMISA is not simply having both insomnia and sleep apnea at the same time. It's a distinct clinical entity where the two conditions interact in ways that make each worse. Insomnia is characterized by difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early, despite adequate opportunity for sleep. Sleep apnea involves repeated pauses in breathing during sleep, leading to oxygen desaturation and frequent arousals. When both are present, the sleep architecture becomes a battlefield: the apnea fragments sleep, causing awakenings that fuel insomnia's hyperarousal, while insomnia's heightened vigilance makes the brain more sensitive to the micro-arousals caused by apnea events.

Prevalence studies suggest that between 30% and 50% of patients with OSA also meet criteria for insomnia, and conversely, a substantial portion of insomnia patients have undiagnosed OSA. Yet, in clinical practice, many patients are treated for only one condition. A patient might receive a CPAP machine for apnea, but their insomnia persists because the underlying hyperarousal isn't addressed. Or a patient might be prescribed sedatives for insomnia, which can worsen apnea by relaxing the airway muscles. This fragmented approach fails because it ignores the bidirectional relationship.

The Science of the Vicious Cycle: How Insomnia and Apnea Fuel Each Other

To understand COMISA, we must look at the neurobiological mechanisms that link the two. Sleep apnea triggers a cascade of physiological responses: intermittent hypoxia (low oxygen), sympathetic nervous system activation, and systemic inflammation. These factors increase arousal thresholds and promote a state of hyperarousal—the hallmark of insomnia. In other words, apnea doesn't just wake you up; it primes your brain to stay awake. Conversely, insomnia is associated with chronic hyperarousal, which can lead to lighter, more fragmented sleep. This makes the upper airway more collapsible during sleep, increasing the likelihood of apnea events. Moreover, the sleep deprivation caused by insomnia can impair the respiratory muscle control and reduce the body's ability to maintain airway patency.

Research using polysomnography (sleep studies) shows that patients with COMISA have more nocturnal awakenings, lower sleep efficiency, and greater oxygen desaturation compared to those with either condition alone. They also exhibit higher levels of cortisol and inflammatory markers, suggesting a compounded physiological stress response. This synergy explains why COMISA is associated with worse outcomes—including higher rates of cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysfunction, depression, and cognitive impairment—than either condition in isolation.

Why COMISA Is Often Missed: The Diagnostic Blind Spot

One of the biggest challenges with COMISA is that its symptoms can mask each other. For example, a person with insomnia might complain of fatigue and difficulty concentrating, which are also symptoms of sleep apnea. Conversely, a person with apnea might attribute their excessive sleepiness to poor sleep from insomnia, never considering that breathing pauses are the root cause. Standard screening tools like the Epworth Sleepiness Scale or the Insomnia Severity Index often fail to capture the overlap. Moreover, many sleep clinics focus on a single diagnosis—if a patient meets criteria for OSA, they may not be thoroughly evaluated for insomnia, and vice versa.

Another diagnostic pitfall is the timing of symptoms. In COMISA, insomnia symptoms often persist even after CPAP treatment for apnea is initiated. This is because the hyperarousal has become entrenched, and the brain's sleep-witch mechanisms have been disrupted. Without recognizing this, clinicians might assume the CPAP isn't working or that the patient is non-adherent, when in fact the insomnia component requires separate intervention. Conversely, treating insomnia with cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT-I) without addressing underlying apnea can lead to worsening respiratory events, as improved sleep may reduce the body's compensatory mechanisms.

Breaking the Cycle: Integrated Treatment Approaches That Work

The good news is that COMISA is treatable, but it requires a comprehensive, integrated strategy. The first step is accurate diagnosis—ideally with a home sleep test or in-lab polysomnography, combined with a thorough clinical interview that assesses both insomnia and apnea symptoms. Once diagnosed, the treatment plan should address both components simultaneously.

For the sleep apnea component, positive airway pressure (PAP) therapy remains the gold standard. However, adherence is a major issue—many patients with COMISA find CPAP uncomfortable and discontinue use. This is where the insomnia treatment becomes crucial. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) has been shown to improve sleep quality and reduce hyperarousal, which in turn can improve CPAP tolerance. A 2022 randomized controlled trial found that combining CBT-I with CPAP was more effective than CPAP alone in reducing insomnia severity and improving adherence.

Other adjunctive strategies include:

  • Positional therapy: For some patients, apnea is worse on the back. Using a positional device or a specialized pillow can reduce apnea events, complementing CPAP.
  • Oral appliances: Mandibular advancement devices can be an alternative for mild-to-moderate apnea, especially when CPAP is not tolerated.
  • Stimulus control: This CBT-I technique helps re-associate the bed with sleep, reducing the anxiety that fuels insomnia.
  • Sleep restriction therapy: Limiting time in bed to match actual sleep time can consolidate sleep and increase sleep drive, countering the fragmentation caused by apnea.
  • Lifestyle modifications: Weight loss, exercise, and avoiding alcohol and sedatives are essential, as these can worsen both conditions.

Practical Steps for Patients and Clinicians

If you suspect you might have COMISA, here are actionable steps to take. First, keep a detailed sleep diary for two weeks, noting not just how long you sleep but also any breathing pauses, gasping, or awakenings. Share this with your healthcare provider. Second, ask for a comprehensive sleep study that measures respiratory events and sleep architecture, not just a basic screening. Third, if you're already on CPAP and still struggling with insomnia, don't accept it as "just the way it is"—request a referral to a sleep psychologist or a provider trained in CBT-I.

For clinicians, the takeaway is to adopt a dual-screening approach. Every patient with insomnia should be asked about snoring, witnessed apnea, and daytime sleepiness. Every patient with OSA should be screened for insomnia symptoms, particularly difficulty falling asleep or early morning awakenings. When both are present, treat them concurrently. This may involve coordinating care between a sleep physician and a behavioral sleep specialist, but the payoff is substantial—improved sleep, better adherence to therapy, and reduced risk of long-term complications.

Conclusion

COMISA represents a paradigm shift in how we understand sleep disorders. It reminds us that sleep is not a simple on-off switch, but a complex interplay of physiological and psychological processes. When insomnia and sleep apnea coexist, they create a feedback loop that can spiral out of control. But by recognizing the signs and embracing an integrated treatment approach, you can break the cycle. Whether you're a patient struggling with persistent sleep problems or a clinician seeking to improve outcomes, the science is clear: treating one without the other is a recipe for failure. Instead, look for the overlap, address both fronts, and reclaim the restorative sleep your body and mind deserve.
