The Silent Sleep Thief: Unmasking Upper Airway Resistance Syndrome

2026-05-18 · Sleep Disorders · Tags: sleepsciencehub, sleep disorders, The

You’ve tried every sleep hygiene tip in the book—blackout curtains, white noise machines, a strict 10 p.m. bedtime. You’ve even cut out caffeine after noon. Yet, you still wake up exhausted, with a foggy brain and a subtle sense that something is off. Your partner says you don’t snore loudly, and your sleep tracker shows you’re getting eight hours. So why do you feel like you’ve been run over by a truck? The answer might not be insomnia, stress, or poor sleep hygiene. It could be a condition that’s been hiding in plain sight: Upper Airway Resistance Syndrome (UARS). This lesser-known sleep-disordered breathing condition affects millions, yet it’s frequently misdiagnosed as chronic fatigue, anxiety, or even treatment-resistant insomnia. In this article, we’ll pull back the curtain on UARS—what it is, why it’s so often missed, and how you can finally get the restorative sleep your body craves.

What Is Upper Airway Resistance Syndrome?

Upper Airway Resistance Syndrome is a form of sleep-disordered breathing that sits on a spectrum between simple snoring and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). In UARS, your airway narrows during sleep—but not enough to cause a complete collapse (apnea) or even a significant drop in oxygen levels (hypopnea). Instead, the increased resistance forces your respiratory system to work harder to pull in air. This effort triggers frequent, brief arousals from sleep—often so short that you don’t consciously wake up, but they fragment your sleep architecture and prevent you from reaching the deep, restorative stages.

First described in the early 1990s by Dr. Christian Guilleminault, UARS was initially thought to be a milder version of sleep apnea. However, research has since shown that it’s a distinct condition with its own pathophysiology. Unlike OSA, where the airway collapses and oxygen desaturations are common, UARS is characterized by increasing respiratory effort that leads to electroencephalographic (EEG) arousals—micro-wakeups that your brain registers but you don’t remember. These arousals can occur dozens or even hundreds of times per night, leaving you exhausted despite spending enough time in bed.

What makes UARS particularly insidious is that it often affects younger, non-obese individuals—especially women—who don’t fit the typical sleep apnea profile. If you’re thin, don’t snore loudly, and have no obvious risk factors, your doctor might never think to test you for a breathing disorder. But the consequences are real: chronic fatigue, cognitive impairment, mood disturbances, and an increased risk of cardiovascular issues over time.

Why UARS Mimics Insomnia and Chronic Fatigue

One of the biggest reasons UARS goes undetected is that its symptoms overlap almost perfectly with insomnia and chronic fatigue syndrome. You might lie in bed for hours, unable to fall asleep, or wake up multiple times during the night and struggle to get back to sleep. You might also experience daytime sleepiness, brain fog, and a feeling of unrefreshing sleep—classic signs of insomnia. But here’s the twist: the root cause isn’t a hyperactive mind or a broken circadian rhythm; it’s your airway struggling to stay open.

When your airway narrows, your brain senses the increased effort and sends out a burst of adrenaline to wake you up just enough to take a deeper breath. This adrenaline surge not only fragments your sleep but also activates your sympathetic nervous system—the “fight or flight” response. Over time, this can lead to chronic hyperarousal, making it even harder to fall asleep and stay asleep. You might find yourself waking up with a racing heart, feeling anxious, or experiencing night sweats. These symptoms are often misattributed to stress or anxiety, and you might be prescribed antidepressants or sleep aids that don’t address the underlying issue.

Another reason UARS is mistaken for insomnia is that the arousals are so brief that you don’t remember them. You might think you slept through the night, but your sleep study would reveal a pattern of frequent awakenings lasting only a few seconds. This is why a standard home sleep test—which often only measures oxygen levels and breathing pauses—can miss UARS entirely. If you’ve been told you have “mild sleep apnea” or “no sleep apnea” but still feel terrible, UARS should be on your radar.

The Physiology Behind the Resistance

To understand UARS, it helps to think of your airway as a flexible tube. During sleep, the muscles that keep this tube open naturally relax. In people with UARS, the airway becomes narrow enough to create turbulence and resistance, but not enough to fully collapse. This resistance increases the work of breathing, and your diaphragm has to work harder to generate the negative pressure needed to pull air in. That increased effort triggers a reflex in your brain: it wakes you up just enough to increase muscle tone and open the airway.

These micro-arousals are the body’s protective mechanism—they prevent you from suffocating. But they come at a cost. Each arousal disrupts the natural progression of sleep stages, particularly the transition into deep slow-wave sleep and REM sleep. You might spend a normal amount of time in bed, but your sleep architecture is shattered. You’re getting mostly light sleep (Stage 1 and 2), which is not restorative. This explains why you can sleep eight hours and still feel like you haven’t slept at all.

Additionally, the repeated sympathetic activation from these arousals can lead to elevated blood pressure, increased heart rate, and even insulin resistance over time. Research has linked UARS to hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and an increased risk of heart disease—even in the absence of significant oxygen desaturations. The condition is not benign, and it deserves the same level of attention as sleep apnea.

Diagnosis: Why Standard Tests Often Miss It

If you suspect UARS, the first step is to seek a comprehensive evaluation from a sleep specialist. The gold standard for diagnosis is an in-lab polysomnography (PSG) that includes esophageal pressure monitoring or, more commonly, a measurement called the Respiratory Effort-Related Arousal (RERA). A RERA is defined as a sequence of breaths characterized by increasing respiratory effort that leads to an arousal from sleep, but does not meet the criteria for an apnea or hypopnea. Unfortunately, many sleep labs and home tests do not routinely score RERAs, which is why UARS is so often missed.

When you book a sleep study, be proactive. Ask the lab whether they score RERAs and whether they use a nasal pressure transducer (which is more sensitive than a thermistor) to detect airflow limitation. You might also want to ask about the use of a continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) trial during the study to see if it eliminates the arousals. Some labs use a technique called “flow limitation analysis” to identify subtle changes in the airflow waveform that indicate increased resistance.

If you’ve already had a sleep study that came back “normal” but you still have symptoms, don’t be afraid to ask for a second opinion. Many patients with UARS are told they have “primary insomnia” or “idiopathic hypersomnia” and are sent home with sleeping pills. But sleeping pills can actually worsen UARS by further relaxing the airway muscles. A proper diagnosis is essential for effective treatment.

Treatment Options: From CPAP to Myofunctional Therapy

Once diagnosed, UARS is highly treatable. The most effective treatment is positive airway pressure therapy, typically CPAP or BiPAP. By splinting the airway open with a gentle stream of air, CPAP eliminates the resistance and prevents the micro-arousals. Many UARS patients find that they need a lower pressure than those with OSA, and some do better with a pressure that adjusts to their breathing pattern (auto-CPAP). If you’ve tried CPAP and found it uncomfortable, consider working with a sleep specialist to optimize the mask fit and pressure settings—it can make a world of difference.

For those who can’t tolerate CPAP, oral appliances that advance the lower jaw (mandibular advancement devices) can be effective, especially if the resistance is due to a narrow palate or retrognathia. These devices work by pulling the tongue and soft palate forward, increasing the airway space. A dentist trained in sleep medicine can fit you for one.

Beyond these, there are adjunctive therapies that can help:

  • Myofunctional therapy: A series of exercises that strengthen the tongue and upper airway muscles. Studies show that these exercises can reduce airway collapsibility and improve symptoms in some patients.
  • Positional therapy: If your UARS is worse when you sleep on your back, using a wedge pillow or a tennis ball sewn into the back of your shirt can help keep you on your side.
  • Weight management and nasal breathing: Even a small amount of weight loss can reduce airway resistance. Additionally, treating nasal congestion with saline rinses or nasal steroid sprays can improve airflow and reduce the effort needed to breathe.
  • Avoiding alcohol and sedatives: These substances relax the airway muscles and can worsen UARS. Cutting back, especially in the evening, can significantly reduce symptoms.

It’s also worth noting that some patients with UARS have a narrow maxilla (upper jaw) and may benefit from orthodontic expansion, particularly if they are younger. This is a more invasive option, but it can be curative in certain cases.

Conclusion

Upper Airway Resistance Syndrome is a silent thief that robs you of restorative sleep without leaving obvious clues. If you’ve been struggling with chronic fatigue, insomnia, or unrefreshing sleep—and you’ve been told you don’t have sleep apnea—it’s time to consider UARS. The good news is that once diagnosed, it’s highly treatable. By advocating for a comprehensive sleep study that includes RERA scoring, you can finally get the answers you need. Don’t let another night go by feeling like you’re running on empty. Your sleep—and your health—deserve a closer look.
